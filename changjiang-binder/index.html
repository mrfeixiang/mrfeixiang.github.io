02

Where to begin

Anthropic × Adaptyv

Claude-support applications close 24 September. Open the application form and check the official terms first. The open track starts 28 September.

Track 1 offers selected experienced teams reserved testing, approximately 18 designs per challenge. Tracks 2 and 3 have no guaranteed testing allocation. Track 2 is for affiliated individuals or teams of up to three.

Eligibility is expressed through legal residence and entity domicile, rather than a blanket statement about Chinese nationality or IP addresses. Korean-based researchers should assess their own situation against the full terms.

Participants retain design ownership, but tested sequences and measurements are published. Check institutional disclosure requirements before submitting.

Poreva Open

The organizer’s 22 September announcement directs readers to MEGAROBO’s official WeChat account for future registration details. A live application form, exact deadlines, target list, and final scoring rules were not linked there when checked.

Bookmark the announcement and revisit the published rules before choosing a submission strategy.

03

Our voyage

Somewhere between a line of code and a clear tube of liquid, an idea has to become a molecule.

That is the journey behind the Changjiang Binder Design Campaign.

We want to design proteins that recognize and bind chosen targets. We begin with sequences on a screen. The goal is to make something we can produce, measure, and trust at the laboratory bench.

It sounds simple until you try it.

Proteins are built mainly from twenty standard amino acids, each represented by a letter. Arrange those letters differently and you change the possibilities: how a chain folds, which surfaces it presents, what it might bind. A binder is a molecular partner designed or selected to recognize a target. Finding the right partner means getting both shape and chemistry to work together.

AI gives us new ways to explore that enormous space. Published work on RFdiffusion and BindCraft has shown that computationally designed proteins can bind their intended targets in experiments. [5] [6] That makes the voyage possible. Each new design still has to earn our confidence.

In the beginning, there was no road.
Only sequences,
like stars falling into a dark sea.

Our campaign begins there, with a question and a willingness to set sail.

On a computer, a protein can look magnificent. The backbone curves into place. Side chains gather around an interface. The target and binder seem made for each other.

Then comes the experiment.

A candidate may be difficult to produce. It may aggregate. It may show no convincing binding at all. The image that looked so persuasive at midnight becomes a result we have to reconsider in the morning.

Anyone who has tried to build something new knows that feeling.

Generate. Fold. Dock. Score. Reject. Again.

The rhythm changes with the workflow. The need to question it stays.

Our poem calls the scores a siren song. It is an image worth keeping close. Structure-confidence estimates and interface scores help us choose candidates to test. Their promise has to survive contact with a physical sample.

So we tie ourselves to the experiment.

Can we express and purify the protein? Does size-exclusion chromatography, or SEC, suggest a suitable sample or reveal larger species that need investigation? Do binding measurements such as BLI or SPR show a reproducible interaction? Does that interaction hold up against appropriate controls?

These are the questions that give the next round of design its direction.

The name Changjiang, the Yangtze River, gives us another image: many tributaries joining the same current.

Someone understands the code. Someone sees a problem in the structure. Someone knows why a purification went wrong. Someone asks the question everyone else has stopped asking.

We need that exchange. We want a failed experiment to travel back to the person writing the next design, carrying enough detail to change what happens next.

Design. Build. Test. Learn.

Our Ithaca, the destination we are working toward, is a moment of evidence. A sequence we designed becomes a protein we can make. It meets its intended target. The measurements support the interaction. We repeat the experiment, and the result holds.

Picture that moment.

For weeks, the molecule existed as letters, coordinates, arguments, and hope. Now it is there in the tube. Something we imagined has acquired a physical existence, and a measurable relationship with another molecule.

That is enough to make us want to set sail again.

If you write code, study proteins, run experiments, or simply wonder how an idea becomes something real, follow the Changjiang journey. Share this story with someone whose skills or curiosity could meet ours. Bring a question you think we should be asking.

There is room for many kinds of minds on this voyage.

Until the horizon takes shape.
Until imagination becomes matter.
